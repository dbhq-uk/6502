# 1 October 2026: the KIM-1

The KIM-1 is MOS Technology's 1976 board: a 6502 at 1 MHz, two 6530 chips
(each a ROM, a little RAM, two I/O ports and a timer), 1 KB of RAM, a hex
keypad and six LED digits. It is the first machine on the core, and the first
that can count towards "machines implemented". It does not count yet: a
machine counts when it runs in the browser as well as passing its test in CI,
and the browser page is the next piece of work. Until then the registry shows
it as in progress.

## What was built

- **`src/Dbhq.Machines.Kim1/`**, a library that depends only on the core.
  `Rriot6530` is the 6530: ROM, RAM, two ports with data direction registers,
  and the interval timer. `Kim1Bus` is the address decoding and the clock:
  every read or write is one cycle, and both 6530s are clocked at the start of
  it. `Kim1Keypad` is the key matrix, `Kim1Display` the six digits, and
  `Kim1Machine` puts them together with the RS and ST keys and the SST switch.
- **`tests/Dbhq.Machines.Kim1.Tests/`**, with `Kim1AcceptanceTests` as the
  suite the registry names.
- **The registry entry** for `kim-1`: maker, rights, notes, the acceptance
  suite, and the status `in-progress`.

Nothing in the core changed. It still names no machine.

## The ROM

### Finding it

The monitor is 2 KB: 1 KB in the 6530-003 at `$1800-$1BFF` (the cassette
routines) and 1 KB in the 6530-002 at `$1C00-$1FFF` (the keypad, display and
teletype monitor). Four copies were downloaded and compared:

- MAME's KIM-1 driver (`src/mame/commodore/kim1.cpp` in `mamedev/mame`) does
  not ship the ROM; it records its hashes. 6530-002: CRC `2b08e923`, SHA-1
  `054f7f6989af3a59462ffb0372b6f56f307b5362`. 6530-003: CRC `a2a56502`, SHA-1
  `60b6e48f35fe4899e29166641bac3e81e3b9d220`.
- `https://www.zimmers.net/anonftp/pub/cbm/src/kim-1/` (`6530-002.bin` and
  `6530-003.bin`, dated 1981) and the `brainwagon/kim-1` repository on GitHub,
  at commit `2daa8c8104315374dabda40855e2d668c13354b7`, both matched MAME's
  hashes exactly. brainwagon's `ORIG.bin` is the two halves joined, 003 first.
- Hans Otten's KIM-1 ROMs page,
  `http://retro.hansotten.nl/6502-sbc/kim-1-manuals-and-software/kim_1-roms/`,
  offers two versions of each half. He writes that the copies found all over
  the internet are the result of assembling the source, with `$FF` in the
  unused bytes, and that "the original KIM-1 ROMs have a filler byte $00,
  verified by me dumping the real 6530-002 and 6530-003 RRIOT ROMs". His `$FF`
  files match MAME's hashes. His `$00` files are different files.

`cmp -l` on the two versions, on 1 October, found 3 bytes different in the
6530-002 and 356 in the 6530-003, and every one of them was `$00` in his dump
against `$FF` in the assembled image. The code is identical.

### What was chosen

**Hans Otten's `$00` dumps, of the real chips.** Dan asked for the original
ROM, and these are the bytes the chips hold; the `$FF` images are what an
assembler makes from the published source. No program can tell the two apart
by running the monitor, but a program that reads an unused byte can.

**Pinned to the Internet Archive's copy of his files, not his site.** His site
answers over plain HTTP, but an HTTPS request fails before any data is sent
(`curl` error 35, "sslv3 alert handshake failure", on 1 October). The Wayback
Machine's raw copies (`id_`) of 23 June 2025 serve the same bytes over HTTPS
from a URL that never changes:

| Chip | URL | SHA-256 |
|---|---|---|
| 6530-002 | `https://web.archive.org/web/20250623140338id_/http://retro.hansotten.nl/uploads/files/6530-002%20fillerbyte00.bin` | `e9e5245854603cdbc0208235310db1bf6e6a75904960385baaddd38fe60ef750` |
| 6530-003 | `https://web.archive.org/web/20250623140339id_/http://retro.hansotten.nl/uploads/files/6530-003%20fillerbyte00.bin` | `f112a707188a82b87de5be78b7ffa014e18240aa08825055c90d2e6626092fd7` |

Both were checked on 1 October against his live HTTP files with `sha256sum`,
and matched. For anyone comparing with other emulators: their CRC-32s are
`d63aa9be` (002) and `9479e729` (003), and their SHA-1s
`d0b5ceb279303cad219d6609d7db03a120f59ab8` and
`16caaf9d4c8327e0d3198887499fe30056df3084`.

The pins live in `tests/Dbhq.Cpu6502.TestSupport/Pins.cs` beside every other
third-party file, and are fetched through the same `PinnedFiles.Fetch`, into
`.testdata/kim-1/`. That was chosen over keeping them in the machine library
because it reuses the one mechanism there is, and because the CI cache key
already hashes `Pins.cs`, so a new pin downloads afresh without anyone
remembering to change the key.

## Rights

Rule 4 in `AGENTS.md`, Dan's decision of 1 October 2026: a machine's system
ROM is used when its rights are documented. This is that documentation, from
what was found while building. A `legwork` research pass was started and
stopped on Dan's instruction.

- **Who wrote it.** MOS Technology. The program listings in the KIM-1 User
  Manual carry "COPYRIGHT MOS TECHNOLOGY, INC DATE: OCT 18, 1975 REV-D" for the
  6530-003 and "COPYRIGHT MOS TECHNOLOGY INC. DATE OCT 13 1975 REV E" for the
  6530-002, and the manual itself "© MOS TECHNOLOGY, INC. 1976 All Rights
  Reserved" (second edition, August 1976, read in the transcription at
  `http://retro.hansotten.nl/uploads/6502docs/usrman.htm`).
- **What happened to MOS.** From `https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MOS_Technology`:
  Commodore bought MOS on 25 September 1976, and MOS became the Commodore
  Semiconductor Group. After Commodore's bankruptcy in 1994 the group was
  bought by its former management and traded as GMT Microelectronics, which
  "discontinued operations and abandoned all of its assets" in 2000.
- **Who holds the copyright now: not established.** Nothing opened today says
  whether it went with the semiconductor group or with Commodore's other
  assets, or where either went after that.
- **Permission: none found from any holder.** No licence, permission or
  statement of policy from MOS, Commodore, or a successor was found.
- **What others say, none of it a licence.** Ruud Baltissen's copyright page,
  `http://www.baltissen.org/newhtm/copyrights.htm`, says of his site's
  Commodore material that "the holders of the brand name don't mind as long
  as it is not used for commercial purposes", with no source given. The
  `brainwagon/kim-1` README adds "no special restrictions" of its own, "consistent with any pre-existing licensing", which covers only that
  author's work. Hans Otten's ROMs page states no copyright or licence. A web
  search summary attributed a statement about a later owner's policy to a
  copy of Baltissen's page; the page as opened today does not contain it, so
  it is not relied on.
- **Where our copy comes from.** Hans Otten's dump of the real chips, through
  the Internet Archive URLs and hashes above. It is downloaded at test time,
  checked against its hash and kept in the git-ignored `.testdata/`. It is
  never committed.

Under rule 4, that is documented, and the ROM is used. The registry's
`rights` field says the same in two sentences, and the machine's page will
say whose ROM it runs.

## What was read

- **The KIM-1 User Manual.** The text transcription at Hans Otten's site
  (above) for the memory map (section 3.2), the decoder (section 6.1), the
  keys and switches (chapter 4) and the 6530's timer (Appendix H). The
  scanned second edition, `KIM-1 User Manual 15A.pdf` on the same site, to
  check one step of the first program (see below).
- **The MCS6530 data sheet**, March 1976, from the Internet Archive item
  `mos-6530` (`MOS6530_djvu.txt`, contributed by Andy Finkel): the ports, the
  pull-ups, the timer, the RES line and the address decoding.
- **Ruud Baltissen's KIM-1 page**, `http://www.baltissen.org/newhtm/kim.htm`:
  the full decode of `$1400-$17FF`, including that `$1400-$16FF` holds
  nothing, that each 64-byte I/O area repeats its 16 registers four times, and
  that single step is masked over the upper half of the ROM.
- **The monitor's own source**, in the manual's listing and in the
  `brainwagon/kim-1` cleanup, for what it does with the ports: `SCAND` at
  `$1F19`, `CONVD`, `AK` and `GETKEY`. That is where the keypad wiring was
  read from.
- **MAME's KIM-1 driver**, as a cross-check of facts only (it is GPL, and no
  code was taken): its key rows agree with the wiring read from `GETKEY`, its
  memory map with the decode above, and its single-step NMI with K7 masking.

## Decisions

- **Two 6530s as one class, created twice,** over a class per chip. The
  6530-002 and 6530-003 differ only in their mask: what is in the ROM, and
  which value of A6 selects each. That difference lives in the bus.
- **The bus clocks both 6530s at the start of every access,** before serving
  it, over clocking them after. A write that starts the timer then lands after
  that cycle's tick, so the first decrement falls on the next clock, which is
  what the data sheet's example needs.
- **The timer passes zero on clock N x k + 1 after the write,** over the
  data sheet's Figure 5, which says clock 416 for 52 at divide by 8. The data
  sheet's own text says 417, and its two later readings agree with 417. Both
  are in `docs/known-differences.md`.
- **A read of the timer takes the interrupt enable from A3,** as the data
  sheet says for every read and write, over Appendix H, which says a read
  after zero disables it from either address. Also in known differences.
  Nothing on a stock KIM-1 wires the timer's interrupt to the CPU, so the
  monitor does not depend on it.
- **The keypad is wired as `GETKEY` decodes it:** row r holds keys 7r to
  7r + 6, the first on PA6 and the last on PA0, so each key's number is the
  code the monitor returns for it. Chosen over a table copied from anywhere,
  because the board only works if wiring and ROM agree, and the acceptance
  test proves they do.
- **The display models the eye, not the LEDs.** A digit shows each segment
  that was on for more than half of the time it was selected, and goes dark
  20 ms after its last scan. Chosen over "the last value written", which
  reads blank: `CONVD` turns the segments off before it moves to the next
  digit, so the last value on every digit is nothing.
- **The display is read from the segments, never from RAM.** The monitor keeps
  the address it shows in RAM at `$FA` and `$FB`. A test that read those
  would pass with a broken scan. The tests read the digits.
- **Single step raises NMI on the opcode fetch outside K7** (`$1C00-$1FFF`),
  over stepping every fetch. The board masks the monitor's half of the ROM so
  that the NMI handler is not itself stepped. The machine marks the first
  access of each instruction as the fetch, since the core has no SYNC line.
- **RAM starts as zeros, and the machine waits for RS,** as the manual says to
  press RS after power on. Zeros were chosen over random contents so the boot
  test can say exactly what the digits show.
- **A read where nothing answers returns the last byte on the data bus.**
  This is the usual NMOS behaviour and was not measured on a KIM-1; it is
  listed as modelled in known differences.
- **Out of scope:** the teletype interface (the machine is always in keypad
  mode: PA0 stays high, as with no teletype jumper fitted), the cassette
  interface and the expansion bus. The registry's notes say so.
- **The class is `Kim1Machine`,** not `Kim1`, which would share its name with
  its own namespace and make every test file say `Dbhq.Machines.Kim1.Kim1`.
- **The status is `in-progress`, not `running`.** The site counts a machine
  only when it runs in the browser, and that page does not exist yet.

## The acceptance test

`Kim1AcceptanceTests` drives the machine the way a person does: a key held
for 40 ms and let go for 40 ms, the digits read off their segments.

- **Boot.** Before RS the digits are dark. After it the monitor shows `0000
  00`, the address in its pointer and the byte there, and a digit key goes
  into the address, so it is in address mode.
- **The User Manual's first program**, section 2.4, typed in exactly as the
  manual lists it, steps 1 to 23, with the display the manual prints checked
  after every step, x for "don't care" included. GO runs it, it adds 02 and
  03, and the address shown is `0005`, as the manual says: "the result, 05,
  appears in the right two digits of the address display". This was chosen
  over a program of our own because the manual gives an outside answer for
  every step.
- **Deposit, run, stop, read back.** The NMI vector set to `$1C00` by keypad
  (manual, section 4.6), a program that loads, adds, stores and loops, GO, the
  digits dark while it runs, ST, the stop address `020A` shown, then the
  result read off the digits at `0212`, from the saved accumulator at `00F3`,
  and the PC key recalling `020A`.
- **Single step**: with SST on, each GO shows the next instruction's address
  and byte.
- **The scan, not memory**: a program that loops forever leaves the digits
  dark although the address is still in RAM.

The other suites check the 6530 against the data sheet's worked example and at
every prescale, the memory map and its mirrors, the ports against their
direction registers, the RS key resetting the ports, and a five-million-cycle
run of the monitor with the keypad in use on which every instruction fetched
is a documented opcode inside the ROM.

## What the tests showed

- `dotnet test tests/Dbhq.Machines.Kim1.Tests -c Release`, on 1 October, after
  the last change: 35 passed, 0 failed.
- **Every assertion was shown to fail when its behaviour was broken.** A
  script applied one change to the machine at a time in a scratch copy,
  rebuilt it and ran the KIM-1 tests: 30 changes, each named for the
  behaviour it breaks (the prescale after zero, the first decrement, divide by
  1024, the flag on the clock it is set, the flag read, the ports' direction
  registers, A3, RES, the 8 KB mirror, writes clocking the timers, RAM and I/O
  swapped, the two chips swapped, `$1400-$16FF`, open bus, SST's K7 mask, SST
  on the fetch, ST, the key columns and rows, the digit order, persistence,
  the half-the-time rule, reading the display from RAM, one wrong ROM byte, a
  JAM in the display routine, and the two ROMs swapped). After one test was
  added, all 30 were caught.
- **The one that got through at first** was RS not resetting the 6530s:
  nothing checked it, because the monitor sets up its own chip's ports again
  after every reset. A test now writes `$FF` to the user port's direction
  register with the keypad, presses RS, and reads `00` back off the digits.
- The whole suite: `dotnet test --configuration Release --logger trx
  --results-directory <folder>` at the repository root, on 1 October, ended
  with 35 of 35 passing in the KIM-1 tests and 1,480 of 1,480 in the core's.

## Surprises and mistakes

- **The test results file was being overwritten.** The workflows ran
  `dotnet test` with `--logger "trx;LogFileName=results.trx"`. With one test
  project that was fine; with two, both write the same file, and the run said
  `WARNING: Overwriting results file`. The site's figures would have lost one
  project's suites without failing anything. The logger is now given no file
  name, and writes one file per project; a probe showed it adds `[1]` and
  `[2]` to a name rather than overwrite one. Both workflows and
  `make-results.mjs` changed, and the script now clears old `.trx` files
  before a fresh run, because it counts every file in the folder.
- **`AD` is a key and a byte.** The first key script wrote the program's
  `LDA absolute` (`$AD`) as `AD`, which pressed the AD key: the display read
  `0B02 0B` where `020C 02` was expected. Key names are now in brackets, as
  the manual prints them on keycaps: `[AD] 0200 [DA] AD`.
- **The text copy of the manual drops two keys.** Its step 21 reads `[DA]`
  and the display `0000 02`. The scan of the manual shows `[DA] [0] [2]`.
- **zimmers.net labels `6530-002.bin` "the lower ROM".** The 6530-002 is the
  upper half, at `$1C00`. The file is right, and matches MAME's hash; only
  the label is not.
- **The data sheet's text, as OCR'd,** says the I/O registers are addressed
  "when A2 is high", but the KIM-1's own addresses put the ports at A2 low
  (`$1740-$1743`) and the timer at A2 high (`$1744-$174F`). The figure that
  would settle it did not survive the OCR. The board's addresses were
  followed.

## CI no longer depends on anyone else's server

On 2 October 2026 Dan ruled that CI must not depend on external sources, and that this is a rule for everything the tests and the build fetch, not only the KIM-1 ROM. Until then three kinds of third party sat in the path: GitHub repositories we did not own (Harte's SingleStepTests, Dormann's tests, `nestest`), `perfect6502` for the generator, and the Internet Archive for the KIM-1 ROM, which had a single source.

**What changed.** The four GitHub repositories are now forks in the `dbhq-uk` organisation (`65x02`, `6502_65C02_functional_tests`, `nes-test-roms`, `perfect6502`), and the ROM is in a new repository, `dbhq-uk/6502-roms`, with its provenance, hashes and rights position written beside it. Every pin points there. The commits and hashes did not change, so nothing about what is checked changed, only where it is fetched from.

**How it was checked.**
- `gh api repos/dbhq-uk/<repo>/commits/<sha>` returned each of the four pinned commits from its fork.
- A Harte file fetched from the fork (`nes6502/v1/a9.json`) hashed with `git hash-object` to `d1f3288004da3d0f2869a2c0e6b37f533addf00a`, the blob hash the manifest records for it.
- The cached ROM, Dormann and nestest downloads were deleted and `dotnet test` run again: 53 tests passed (39 KIM-1, 14 Dormann and nestest), and the three folders it downloaded were byte-identical to the earlier ones (`diff -rq`).

**The rule is enforced.** `site/tests/mirrors.test.mjs` scans the test, tool, bench and source folders and the site's scripts and fails on any fetch from a host outside `dbhq-uk` (comments and local addresses are exempt). It also requires every pin to be a full 40-character commit. AGENTS.md rule 3 says all of this.

**What the rule does not cover, honestly.** A fork is still on GitHub, so this removes our dependence on other people's repositories and on the Internet Archive, not on GitHub itself. Package registries (npm, NuGet) and GitHub Actions are locked and pinned as before and are a separate question. The first CI run after this change downloads the 5 GB of Harte data again, from our fork, because the cache key includes the pins file.
